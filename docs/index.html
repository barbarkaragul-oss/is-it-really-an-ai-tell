Is it really an AI tell?

People judge writing by the word delve, the em dash, missing contractions. This page counts each of those in the same documents written by a person and by five language models, and shows the sentences it counted.

★ Star on GitHub How it is counted The data

This is not a detector. Nothing here can tell you who wrote a text, and a text full of these markers proves nothing. The table is mostly evidence of how little they prove.

The markers

Every column below except the last three is the same 1,499 academic abstracts: written by a person, and continued by each model from the same prompt (RAID). A difference between those columns is about the writer, not the subject. Tinted cells are well above or below the person. Click a row to see what was counted.

* Claude was generated for this project (45 abstracts, reached through Claude Code) and the same system wrote this page; the caveats matter. The verdict compares GPT-4 with the person; the placebo check (the person's abstracts split at random) ties on every row.

One paper, six writers

The same prompt, the title of a real paper, answered six ways. Counted markers are highlighted. Documents are drawn at random from the ones every writer covered, not chosen.

Check a text

Paste anything. The markers in it are highlighted and each one is shown next to how often people use it. There is no score, because the table above does not support one. Nothing leaves your browser.

How it is counted

  • Two measures. A word or phrase is counted per thousand words, which does not care how long a text is. A property of the whole text, such as no contractions anywhere, is the share of texts that have it, with each arm matched to the person's abstracts by length.
  • Patterns, shown. Every marker is a regular expression. For every cell the page can show what it matched: the forms, the word in front, and five sentences picked by a seeded shuffle. Sentences are quoted from RAID (MIT) and the Claude arm only; Hacker News and Stack Exchange are linked.
  • A placebo. The person's abstracts are split at random and everything runs on both halves. Every row should tie, and does.
  • Remembered, not written. Asked for the abstract of a real paper, a model can reproduce the published one. Machine texts that share more than half their five-word sequences with the human document are dropped: none of RAID's, five of Claude's fifty.
  • Context arms. Hacker News comments and Stack Exchange answers from before ChatGPT existed, and GPT-3.5 answering questions (HC3). They are a different genre, so they are not tinted; they are there to show how much of a marker is about the kind of document.
